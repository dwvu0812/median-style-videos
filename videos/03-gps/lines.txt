Right now, in your pocket, your phone is running an experiment in relativity. Not a metaphor. A real experiment, with real clocks in orbit, running every single second of every day.
Here is the claim. If Einstein had been wrong, your maps would stop working in about two minutes. Not two days. Two minutes. Then your blue dot would start drifting away from where you actually are.
That sounds impossible. Relativity sounds like black holes and chalkboards, not finding a coffee shop. But by the end of this video, you will see exactly why it is true — with one shape, and two clocks.
First, forget the map. GPS is not a map. GPS is a clock. A whole sky full of clocks, all ticking together, telling your phone the exact time, down to the billionth of a second.
More than thirty satellites orbit twenty thousand kilometres above you. That is twelve thousand five hundred miles. Each one keeps shouting the same message: here is where I am, and here is the exact time I sent this.
Your phone listens to at least four of them at once. For each one, it measures a single thing: how long the signal took to travel from space, all the way down to your hand.
From those four travel times, your phone solves four unknowns. Three are your position — where you are, and how high up. The fourth is the time itself, because the cheap little clock inside your phone is far too sloppy to trust.
Why four satellites, and not three? Three would be enough to find you — if your phone's clock were perfect. It is not. So the fourth satellite is not for position at all. It measures your phone's own clock error, and subtracts it out.
And here is the catch. Those signals travel at the speed of light — three hundred thousand kilometres every second. So the timing has to be absurdly precise. No room for almost. No room for close enough.
In one nanosecond — one billionth of a second — light travels about thirty centimetres. About a foot. The length of a school ruler, covered in less time than a camera shutter takes to open.
So if your phone's clock is wrong by just one nanosecond, your position is wrong by thirty centimetres. Wrong by ten nanoseconds, and you are three metres off. The whole system lives or dies on billionths of a second.
Your phone promises you about five to ten metres of accuracy. Enough to know which side of the street you are on. Enough for your delivery to find your door, and your morning run to be measured right. To keep that promise, it has to time signals to within a few billionths of a second. That is the game. And relativity is trying to break it.
The first problem is speed. Each satellite races around the Earth at almost four kilometres per second — about two and a half miles every second. It circles the whole planet in about twelve hours. Twice a day, every day.
Einstein's special relativity, from nineteen-oh-five, says something strange about speed. A moving clock ticks more slowly than a still one. The faster you go, the slower your time runs. Not the machinery. Time itself.
So from down here on Earth, the satellite's clock looks slow. Not by much. But the numbers are exact, and they never stop adding up. It loses about seven microseconds every single day, day after day, without fail.
Seven microseconds sounds like nothing. Seven parts in a million. But remember the ruler. One microsecond of error is three hundred metres of wrong position. So seven microseconds a day is about two kilometres of error, every day, from speed alone.
That alone would break your maps within a day or two. But speed is only half the story. There is a second effect, and it pushes the clock in exactly the opposite direction.
The second effect comes from Einstein's general relativity, from nineteen fifteen. It says something even stranger. Gravity slows time down. Stronger gravity, slower clock.
Down here on the surface, gravity is strong, so our clocks run slow. Up in orbit, gravity is much weaker, so a clock up there runs fast. Same clock. Different height. Different speed of time.
How much faster? The satellites sit twenty thousand kilometres up, where gravity is far weaker than down here. Their clocks gain about forty-five microseconds every day, just from being high up.
Stop and feel how weird that is. Two identical clocks — one on the ground, one in the sky. Simply by being higher up, the sky clock pulls ahead by forty-five parts in a million of a second a day. Nothing is moving. Nothing is broken. It is just gravity, bending time.
So now we have a tug of war. Speed pulls the satellite's clock slow, by seven microseconds a day. Height pushes it fast, by forty-five. Two effects, both real, both measured, pulling in opposite directions.
Subtract one from the other, and the satellite's clock ends up gaining about thirty-eight microseconds a day. Thirty-eight parts in a million. A number so small you would never notice it in your life. Your phone would notice it in minutes.
Turn that time into distance, and thirty-eight microseconds becomes about eleven kilometres of error every day. About seven miles. Left alone for a week, your maps would put you in the wrong city. Left alone for a month, the wrong country. Your phone would lie to you by eleven kilometres a day — unless Einstein saves it.
That is about eight metres every minute. Twenty-six feet. Ticking upward, minute after minute, without ever stopping, without ever slowing down.
And it never resets itself. Every day adds another eleven kilometres on top of yesterday's. The error does not average out. It accumulates, like a clock that gains a minute a day and is never set right.
Remember the promise: five to ten metres. After one minute, the error is already eight metres. After two minutes, sixteen metres — and the promise is broken. That is the two minutes in the title. Not a blackout. A broken promise.
And notice what fails. The screen does not go black. The map still draws, the blue dot still moves, the voice still says turn left. It just quietly puts you on the wrong street, then the wrong block. Your delivery driver ends up at the wrong door — and you would have no way of knowing, because the phone itself believes its own clock.
So how do engineers fix it? They cheat. And they cheat before the satellite even leaves the ground. The fix is not in software. It is baked into the clock itself, in the hardware.
Every satellite carries an atomic clock built to tick at ten point two three megahertz. But before launch, engineers deliberately set it to run slow. Slow by exactly the right amount, so relativity will speed it up to the correct rate once it is in orbit.
The change is absurdly tiny — less than five thousandths of a hertz. But it is exactly the thirty-eight microseconds a day that Einstein predicted, more than sixty years before the first satellite flew.
Think about what that means. They build a clock that is wrong on Earth, on purpose — because they know it will be right in space. The correction is built in before the rocket ever lights. And it has worked, silently, for decades.
And your phone does its part too. Every time it listens to four satellites, it solves for its own clock error as a fourth unknown. So even the cheap little clock in your pocket gets corrected, second by second, by the sky above you.
This is not just theory. In nineteen seventy-seven, engineers flew a satellite with a switch. Correction on, correction off. With the correction on, the drift vanished. With it off, the clock drifted exactly as Einstein had predicted.
Nothing in the system ignores relativity. The satellite clocks are pre-corrected before launch, the receivers correct their own error, and ground stations watch the drift and upload fresh corrections every single day.
So, one more time. Your phone finds where you are by measuring time. Light moves thirty centimetres in a nanosecond, so a few billionths of a second are the difference between the right street and the wrong one.
Speed slows the satellite's clock by seven microseconds a day. Height speeds it up by forty-five. The net gain is thirty-eight microseconds — eleven kilometres of error a day, eight metres a minute — if nobody corrected it. And somebody did.
The fix is beautifully simple. Build the clock wrong on the ground, so it is right in the sky. Your phone would lie to you by eleven kilometres a day — unless Einstein saves it. And every second, in your pocket, it does.
